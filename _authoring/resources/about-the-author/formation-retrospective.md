# Formation Retrospective

## Overview

This article is a comprehensive post-mortem of Formation, a corporate-backed loyalty platform startup co-founded by the author through BCGDV (Boston Consulting Group Digital Ventures). The company successfully delivered $250M+ of incremental value to Starbucks, achieved $1B+ revenue at scale, but ultimately failed to raise follow-on funding and was acquired by BCG. The piece distills 13 key lessons learned from the startup journey, emphasizing the critical importance of team alignment and shared vision.

---

## Context: The Corporate Venture Model

### BCGDV (Boston Consulting Group Digital Ventures)

**Founding Vision:** Address the corporate innovation paradox—large companies with capital but unable to innovate fast enough to compete with tech giants and VC-backed startups.

**Thesis:** Corporate investment in external, independent startup teams with meaningful ownership stakes would outperform internal innovation efforts.

**Marketing:** Referred to as an "unfair advantage"—hybrid model of BCG's business prowess with design thinking and tech talent.

**Author's Initial Reaction:** Skeptical of the "unfair advantage" framing; felt unethical competitive advantage like "cheating."

**Reality Check:** The term was really about proving that management consultants + creative/tech consultants could out-perform traditional VC-backed founders.

### Author's Path to Formation

**Prior Experience:** Microsoft (Blaise Agüera y Arcas' R&D team)

**Microsoft Observation:** Great ideas got cancelled every six months due to reorganization and executive risk-aversion. Decisions made by project sponsor risk tolerance, not product merit.

**Key Insight from Rick Chavez (Microsoft exec):** Left-brain (business) + right-brain (design) thinking creates valuable hybrids.

**The Call:** Friend at BCGDV invited author to build design cohort for healthcare/med-tech innovation.

**Initial Assessment:** Fantastic opportunity given corporate innovation problems author witnessed at Microsoft.

## The Starbucks Opportunity

### The Pitch (2015)

**Context:** DV delegation discussing personalization with Starbucks senior leadership

**Where Previous DV Ventures Failed:** Used "ideation sprint" approach (3-4 months for conceptual validation)

**Starbucks Difference:** Loved the loyalty program vision AND wanted assurances team could build enterprise-grade product

**The Gamble:** Built a mockup and promised functional prototype in 6 weeks, enterprise-ready product in 6 months

**The Team:** Pulled Ryan and Jason Hickner (brother duo) as core engineering/design leads

### Initial Success

**First Deployment:** 10% of Starbucks loyalty population

**Results:** 300% engagement increase (Starbucks thought measurement was wrong)

**Starbucks' Validation:** Built their own measurements—results were more conservative than initial analysis

**Follow-on Results:** 5x-7x engagement on new offer constructs

**Revenue Impact:** Delivered $200M+ incremental annual revenue to Starbucks (largest single revenue source)

**Shareholder Impact:** Star of shareholder meetings; helped bolster stagnant share price

### The Investment

**Series A:** $25M from Starbucks

**Additional Funding:** $5M from B Capital (BCG-affiliated) at $120M valuation

**Total Raise:** $30M at implied $120-125M valuation

**Ownership:** BCG and Starbucks owned venture; founders had minority stakes (fraction of traditional equity)

**Stated Expectations:**
1. Deliver $1B added value to Starbucks (achieved)
2. Reach $1B valuation (unicorn status) within 5 years (failed)

## The 13 Lessons

### Lesson #1: Management Consulting Built on Relationships, Not Disruption

**Key Finding:** Management consulting is trust-based over many years; introducing "disruptive design thinkers" introduces unknowns.

**Context:** BCGDV promised access to Fortune 500 C-Suite through BCG relationships, but initial implementation faced friction.

**BCG Philosophy:** "Organizations should grow like an oak tree"—slow, steady, deep roots. Not explosive growth.

**Implication:** Corporate venture model conflicts with BCG's organic growth culture.

**Lesson for Founders:** Trust-based relationships take time to build; cannot rush cultural integration.

### Lesson #2: Business Consultants vs. Designers: Mars vs. Venus

**Core Conflict:** Management consultants prioritize quantitative results in Excel; designers prioritize qualitative empathy and synthesis.

**Who Wins:** "Cold, hard actionable quantitative result" beats "deep empathy" when stakeholders are business-thinkers.

**Resolution:** Found harmony when vision was clear and unified—Starbucks pitch succeeded because message was crystalline.

**Lesson for Founders:** When business and design teams conflict, lack clear north star; need alignment before engagement.

### Lesson #3: Pitching Innovation ≠ Building Enterprise Software

**Key Insight:** Ideation and prototyping are fundamentally different from enterprise product development.

**What Worked:** Demonstrating value quickly through mockups and promises of delivery.

**What Failed:** Taking those promises and scaling to enterprise-grade multi-tenant architecture.

**Lesson for Founders:** Scale too early can break a product; validate across customers before scaling.

### Lesson #4: Show Value Quickly to Build Trust

**Approach:** Rather than defend experience and credentials, built working prototype.

**Result:** Shifted conversation from "Can you do this?" to "When can you deliver?"

**Lesson for Founders:** Action beats pontification; bias for action with solid results speaks volumes.

## The Product Journey

### The Original Offering: Holistic Loyalty Platform

**Vision:** End-to-end 1:1 personalized marketing automation with AI/ML

**Components:** Audience segmentation, offer design, deployment, measurement, and optimization

**Key Feature - "Lens":** Simple audience segmentation tool that unlocked rapid experimentation

**Success Metrics:** Dramatically reduced Starbucks' offer deployment time (12 weeks → 1 week)

**Remaining Challenge:** Still required 150+ person-hours per offer to coordinate across teams

**Team Coordination:** Complex coordination between executives, business strategists, data scientists, copywriters, designers, operators

### Lesson #5: Machine Learning at Scale Is Corporate Venture's Greatest Advantage

**Breakthrough:** Using corporate customer's massive dataset for rapid ML training and validation

**Benefit:** Achieved step-change in value and efficiency impossible in traditional startup ramp-up

**Comparison:** Most ML efforts fail before showing impact; had validation from day one at scale

**Lesson for Founders:** Corporate customer scale is genuine competitive advantage; leverage it.

### The Pivot: The Black Box Approach

**Context:** Board mandate to scale to new customers despite product not yet productized

**Problem:** Starbucks was uniquely positioned (saturated market, retention focus) with $3M monthly operational costs delivering $15M monthly value

**The Assumption:** If they scale, other customers just need simplified version

**Lesson #8 (Out of Order):** Sample size of one is not statistically significant

**The Decision:** Move to "black box" AI solution; let reinforcement learning manage complexity

**Rationale:** Vision was for AI to manage multi-variate optimization without human involvement

**Stakeholder Difference:** Starbucks operators understood and could explain decisions; new customers were executives without operational visibility

### Lesson #6: Novel Product Development ≠ Scalable Enterprise Software

**Core Problem:** Team composition, goals, and process for novel ideas differs from scaling existing products

**What Happened:** Hired new leadership with "scale" mandate; shifted from hypothesis validation to immediate scaling

**Consequence:** Lost focus on product refinement; pivoted to premature scaling strategy

**Lesson for Founders:** Changing teams mid-growth creates strategic misalignment; need continuity of vision.

### Lesson #7: Clear Product Vision Anchors Decision-Making

**What Was Missing:** A clear, articulated product mission that everyone understood and committed to

**Consequence:** When conflicts arose about direction, no north star to anchor decisions

**What Happened:** Made choices based on scaling opportunity rather than product coherence

**Lesson for Founders:** Vision clarity > being right; shared vision enables teams to navigate ambiguity.

## The Ethical and Strategic Challenges

### Lesson #9: People Want to Claim Credit for Their Work

**The Problem:** Black box AI removes operator understanding and attribution

**Consequence:** Operators lose sense of ownership; can't explain how decisions are made

**Ethical Issue:** "When you take operational control away, human impact abstracted into short-term, quantitative decisioning"

**The Shift:** From long-term customer happiness concerns to dopamine hits from successful exploits

**Author's Response:** Designed "explainability" tools to expose ML reasoning, but only partially successful

**Lesson for Founders:** Abstraction of human decision-making from operators creates ethical risks; keep humans in the loop.

### The Black Box Failure

**Why It Failed:**
1. Savvy customers don't trust black box solutions
2. Shifted pain to other parts of process rather than solving holistically
3. Forgot that humans are intelligent decision-makers too
4. Seduced by magic of automation without validating with actual users

**Lesson for Founders:** Automation is not always the answer; human judgment has value.

## The Funding Crisis

### Lesson #10: High Series A Valuation Raises Stakes for Future Funding

**The Situation:** Impressive story (Starbucks, United Airlines, $20M+ annual revenue, reliable performance)

**Market Reception:** Expected $50M investment on $250M valuation; got neither

**The Problem:** 80% revenue from one customer at $250M valuation = enormous risk to investors

**Investor Preference:** Hockey-stick growth and familiar patterns; Formation was unique and risky

**The Squeeze:** Couldn't raise money at reasonable valuation

**Lesson for Founders:** High early valuation creates expectations and risk profile that make future funding harder.

### The Board's Reaction

**Knee-Jerk Response:** Pour fuel on marketing; get 20 customers ASAP to reduce concentration risk

**Reality:** Black-box approach wasn't finding market fit; no sales coming in

**The Pivot:** Return to original product (holistic, complex offering) from scratch

**Irony:** Re-building in 2020 what was built in 2015 (six months development cost)

### Lesson #11: Agree and Commit

**The Mistake:** Abandoned initial product vision without market validation of alternative approach

**The Consequence:** Spent years chasing a "shortcut" that didn't work

**Market Recognition:** Analysts named the category "hyper-personalization" while company was internally confused

**Lesson for Founders:** Second-guessing without validation wastes time; commit to vision or validate before pivoting.

## The Governance and Leadership Crisis

### Lesson #12: Leadership Team with Shared Purpose and Vision Is Paramount

**Board Composition:** CEO (ex-BCG consultant), three BCG partners, one ex-BCG consultant (enterprise software exec), Starbucks observer

**Problem:** Impressive business acumen but limited startup experience

**Board Focus:** Growth targets and financial metrics; limited depth on product value and readiness

**Early Warning:** Signs of divergent vision among founders and board members

**Author's Mistake:** Chose to ignore the dissonance

**Critical Insight:** "Even more important than aligned vision is a shared sense of purpose—not only company purpose, also human purpose"

**Historical Context:** Creating companies was multi-generational; tied to community/societal prosperity. Rapid growth and exit model is American idea; may not foster deep purpose.

**Lesson for Founders:** Alignment on vision AND purpose is prerequisite; cannot ignore early dissonance.

---

## The End Game

### Lesson #13: An Exit Doesn't Always Mean Success

**The Fundraising Failure:** Couldn't raise Series B at any reasonable valuation

**Reasons:**
- High valuation and high-risk profile
- Growing competition
- Unconventional corporate ownership (no reputable VC backing)
- "Unfair advantage" became "cautionary tale"

**The Acquisition:** BCG acquired Formation as part of "BCG X" (AI and data services division)

**Author's Feeling:** "Bitter-sweet end to a billion dollar aspiration"

**Lesson for Founders:** Exit to acquirer ≠ success; only validates if aligned with purpose.

## Core Themes Across Lessons

### Alignment Failures

**Where Alignment Was Lost:**
1. Team vision vs. board expectations
2. Novel product development vs. premature scaling
3. Product complexity vs. black-box simplification
4. Initial success vs. scaled ambition

**Consequence of Each Misalignment:** Strategic failure despite tactical success.

### Speed vs. Foundation

**The Pattern:** Initial success from fast, focused execution with clear vision → speed prioritized over foundation in scaling → collapse

**Lesson:** Foundation-building work (product clarity, team alignment, market validation) is not "wasted time"; it's prerequisite for sustainable scaling.

### Founder Equity and Motivation

**The Compromise:** BCG took all risk; founders had fraction of traditional equity

**Author's Ambivalence:** "BCG took all the risk... but founder's stake in success is important factor in outcome"

**The Reality:** Founder motivation (and therefore commitment to mission) was diminished by diluted equity

**Lesson:** Founder equity is not just financial; it's psychological commitment mechanism.

## Cooper's Three Legs of the Stool

**Alan Cooper Framework:** Successful product requires balance of three elements:

| Leg | Owner | Focus |
|-----|-------|-------|
| **Viability** | Business | Revenue, market fit, financial sustainability |
| **Capability** | Engineering | Technical feasibility, architecture, scalability |
| **Desirability** | Design/Product | User value, usability, aesthetic appeal |

**Formation's Problem:** One or more legs regularly out of balance

**Result:** "Without finding balance, which means shared vision, ownership, priority, etc., the stool will not stand."

**Most Lessons Trace Back To:** Failure to maintain three-legged balance.

## Lessons for Current/Future Founders

### What Worked
1. Quick value demonstration (prototype over credentials)
2. Access to large-scale customer data for ML validation
3. Clear initial product vision with strong execution
4. Talented, committed team in early stages
5. Corporate backing providing financial runway

### What Failed
1. Maintaining alignment as company grew
2. Resisting growth pressure when product not ready
3. Losing founder incentive through equity dilution
4. Prioritizing scaling over product foundation
5. Ignoring early warning signs of team misalignment
6. Sample-size-of-one validation of new product direction

### Critical Success Factors Not Present
1. Unified vision across founders and board
2. Shared sense of purpose beyond financial metrics
3. Product-centric decision-making culture
4. Founder equity sufficient to maintain commitment
5. Founder representation on board with real influence

## Broader Implications

### On Corporate Venture Model

**Promise:** Hybrid of corporate capital + startup agility = competitive advantage

**Reality:** Conflicting cultures (oak tree growth vs. hockey-stick growth) create friction

**Outcome:** Neither pure startup nor pure corporate; trapped in middle

**Lesson:** Corporate venture requires genuine cultural integration, not just financial investment.

### On AI Ethics in Products

**The Black Box Problem:** When ML removes human understanding and attribution, ethical issues emerge

**Operator Disempowerment:** Workers can't claim credit for decisions; lose sense of agency

**Exploitation Risk:** Optimization becomes purely extractive without ethical guard rails

**Author's Response:** Advocated publicly for ethical AI frameworks; designed explainability tools

**Lesson:** Automation is not neutral; requires ethical governance.

### On Founder Motivation

**Financial vs. Psychological:** Equity matters not just economically but as commitment mechanism

**Purpose vs. Profit:** Deep sense of mission (beyond financial return) correlates with resilience

**Author's Reflection:** "I'll never ignore early signs of divergent vision again"

**Lesson:** Founder alignment is prerequisite; cannot be "managed around."
